# TriValley.fit Fitness Video Playbook

Read [the Muse handoff](MUSE_VIDEO_HANDOFF.md) and [avatar production runbook](AVATAR_VIDEO_WORKFLOW.md) with this playbook. The existing short-Reel renderer accepts 8–60 seconds and one to ten segments; use the private extended compositor for longer pieces.

## Purpose and audience

Use this reusable workflow to create engaging, evidence-supported fitness videos for TriValley.fit. It carries forward the successful NVDA video's production approach: an immediate hook, a clearly framed question, readable visual evidence, varied footage, expressive August Nick narration, precise captions, and a useful closing action.

The fitness version should feel like Nick coaching a person through one useful idea. Let suspense come from a question the video actually answers. Match the energy to the subject; technique instruction should remain easy to follow.

This document defines a creative production workflow. It does not prescribe a client's training, interpret health data, or claim that a finished fitness video has already been produced.

## Visual and voice identity

### Brand system

- Use a deep navy background, restrained gold accents, clean white text, and the approved Celtic Trinity knot mark. Use a secondary accent sparingly when it clarifies an example or comparison.
- Keep the main title and the key cue readable on a phone. Make the cue larger than supporting source text.
- Preserve a dedicated caption area. Do not cover the demonstrator's feet, hands, joint position, equipment contact, or another body area needed to understand the movement.
- Use the established portrait format as the baseline: 9:16, with the successful workflow's 720 × 1280 output available as a proven delivery option. Higher-resolution exports are acceptable when the source footage supports them.
- Use a small number of clear cards and purposeful cuts. For a movement demonstration, hold the shot long enough for the viewer to see the complete action.
- Make comparisons honest: use the same view, playback speed, movement phase, and labeling when those choices affect the conclusion.

### August Nick narration

Use the authorized August Nick voice through the existing approved production setup. Retrieve asset references from the private runtime configuration; do not place private identifiers in public repository documents.

Delivery should be warm, confident, animated, and easy to understand. Use emphasis and brief pauses around the main cue. Read numbers and exercise names clearly. One short joke can add personality when it does not distract from the instruction or target a person's body, ability, or condition.

Write an original script in Nick's brand voice. Do not present it as another broadcaster's script or performance.

### Nick's avatar and human demonstrations

An approved Nick avatar may introduce the question or deliver a short closing message when that improves the video. The successful workflow used a short speaking introduction; it did not require the avatar to remain on screen throughout the explanation.

Generate the final narration before creating or selecting a speaking avatar segment. Align the avatar to the exact words and timing used in the edit. Cut away at a natural sentence boundary. A still portrait is suitable for a closing card when it avoids an unnecessary speaking segment.

For precise exercise instruction, use reviewed footage of a real person performing the movement. Select a camera angle that makes the relevant action visible. An avatar speaking to the camera can explain a cue; it should not serve as proof that a generated exercise movement is mechanically accurate.

Label illustrative synthetic footage when needed to keep its role clear. Do not present generated footage as a real client session, a documented transformation, or evidence of a person's results.

## Production sequence

### 1. Define the claim and the practical point

Write one sentence stating what the video will help the viewer understand. Separate an observable coaching cue from a claim about physiological effects or outcomes.

For example, a demonstration may show where an elbow moves. A statement that the cue improves a particular outcome requires appropriate evidence. Do not convert a visual preference into a universal medical or performance claim.

### 2. Verify the evidence before locking the script

Use relevant primary research, official professional guidance, or the original manufacturer instructions for equipment-specific claims. Open the sources and record the passages or findings that support the proposed wording.

Maintain a compact claim ledger:

| Claim ID | Proposed wording | Primary source and date | Population or setting | Limits | Approved final wording |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `[C01]` | `[CLAIM]` | `[TITLE + PUBLIC URL]` | `[WHO/WHAT WAS STUDIED]` | `[RELEVANT QUALIFICATION]` | `[SUPPORTED WORDING]` |

Apply the evidence to the population and setting it actually covers. Describe uncertainty where it affects the takeaway. If a claim cannot be supported, narrow or remove it before publication.

For a program introduction, verify the offer against its current source of truth. Confirm the actual contents, intended audience, equipment requirements, duration, price if mentioned, and working destination. Avoid promising outcomes that the product has not established.

### 3. Write for speech and storyboard together

Build the script around this sequence:

1. **Hook:** Give the viewer a concrete question, recognizable problem, or revealing comparison.
2. **Evidence:** Explain what supports the answer in plain language, including a qualification when it changes the interpretation.
3. **Demonstration:** Show the actual movement, relevant equipment, or real program material.
4. **One action:** End with a single next step that matches the viewer and the video's scope.

Use a scene record for every beat:

| Scene field | Requirement |
|---|---|
| ID and heading | A stable scene ID and a short descriptive heading |
| Narration | Exact spoken words, including intentional pauses or delivery notes in separate fields |
| Screen text | The title, cue, or key number the viewer needs |
| Visual | Approved avatar, human demonstration, genuine program material, or clearly illustrative footage |
| Evidence | Claim IDs and public references supporting this scene |
| Timing | Estimate first; update from the finished narration |
| Caption placement | Preserve the part of the frame needed to understand the action |

### 4. Produce the audio and visual assets

Create the expressive August Nick narration from the evidence-checked script. Review pronunciation, pacing, and numerical wording. Use its actual timing to set scene lengths.

Select approved footage and record or request missing human demonstration shots when a precise technique claim depends on them. Do not replace missing movement evidence with a plausible-looking generated demonstration.

Use the avatar only in the shots that benefit from it. Existing authorization for Nick's specific portrait and voice should carry forward according to its documented scope. Obtain any additional rights needed for another person's likeness, voice, or footage before use.

### 5. Edit with mobile viewing in mind

- Keep captions synchronized to the spoken words. Remove delivery instructions from the displayed text.
- Prefer short caption phrases with stable line breaks. Use highlighting when it helps the viewer follow the narration.
- Show the main cue at the moment the movement demonstrates it. A brief freeze frame, replay, or simple arrow can help when the underlying image remains accurate.
- Preserve the full relevant movement. Do not cut away before the viewer can see what the cue changes.
- Keep music below the narration and reduce it automatically while Nick speaks. Preserve a natural pace instead of compressing the voice to force a duration target.
- Use public source labels in the video and place complete references in the accompanying notes.

### 6. Review the finished export

Inspect the actual exported video, not only the source slides or script. Check several representative frames at phone size and listen to the complete audio.

Confirm that the lesson is clear, the movement footage supports the instruction, the captions match the voice, the avatar timing is aligned, numbers and qualifications remain visible, and the closing action matches the brief. Check for missing font glyphs, cropped labels, distracting audio levels, awkward cuts, or text covering the movement.

Keep a contact sheet, the caption file, a scene manifest, and source notes as reproducible production evidence. Describe the checks that were actually completed; do not claim a quality score or verification that was not performed.

### 7. Deliver or publish within the authorized scope

Deliver a playable MP4, a clear poster frame, captions, and concise source notes. Report the main takeaway, the assets used, and any material limitation such as a missing demonstration shot.

Follow the publication scope already authorized by Nick's request or standing mandate. When publication is authorized, complete the review, publish to the specified destination, and verify the result. Otherwise deliver the finished private review copy.

## Three neutral concept structures

These are templates with placeholders, not finished exercise prescriptions.

### Concept A — One Coach Cue

**Working title:** `[ONE CUE] for [MOVEMENT]`

**Suggested structure:** approximately 30–45 seconds, adjusted to the demonstration.

| Beat | Spoken placeholder | Visual requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | “Watch what changes when we focus on [OBSERVABLE DETAIL].” | Show the relevant part of the real movement immediately. |
| Context | “For [DEFINED MOVEMENT OR GOAL], the cue is [PLAIN-LANGUAGE CUE].” | A large, short cue; retain the whole relevant body position. |
| Demonstration | “Here is [FIRST OBSERVABLE POSITION]. Now watch [SPECIFIC CHANGE].” | Reviewed human footage; consistent angle and labeled replay. |
| Qualification | “[ONE LIMIT OR VARIATION THAT MATTERS TO THIS CUE].” | A concise label or alternate reviewed example. |
| One action | “[SAVE THIS CUE / ASK YOUR COACH TO REVIEW THIS DETAIL / VIEW THE LINKED DEMONSTRATION].” | One destination or instruction. |

**Evidence focus:** Support the stated purpose of the cue. Describe the visible movement accurately without claiming a universal correction for every person.

### Concept B — A Fitness Myth Check

**Working title:** “Does [COMMON CLAIM] hold up?”

**Suggested structure:** approximately 40–60 seconds.

| Beat | Spoken placeholder | Visual requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | “You have probably heard [CLAIM]. Here is the part that needs context.” | Show the claim as a question, not an established fact. |
| Evidence | “[PRIMARY SOURCE] examined [POPULATION/SETTING] and found [SUPPORTED RESULT].” | One readable evidence card with a public source label. |
| Demonstration | “In practice, the difference looks like [OBSERVABLE EXAMPLE].” | Real movement footage or an exact, source-based diagram. |
| Takeaway | “For [DEFINED CONTEXT], the useful conclusion is [QUALIFIED ANSWER].” | Show the answer in plain language. |
| One action | “[ONE EDUCATIONAL NEXT STEP].” | A single clear closing card. |

**Evidence focus:** Preserve the study population, comparison, and relevant limitations. Distinguish a measured result from Nick's interpretation or coaching application.

### Concept C — Inside a Program

**Working title:** “What is inside [VERIFIED PROGRAM NAME]?”

**Suggested structure:** approximately 45–75 seconds.

| Beat | Spoken placeholder | Visual requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | “If you want [PROGRAM PURPOSE], here is how [PROGRAM NAME] is organized.” | Optional short Nick avatar introduction plus the actual product. |
| Audience | “It is designed for [VERIFIED INTENDED AUDIENCE], using [ACTUAL EQUIPMENT].” | Real equipment and an accurate audience label. |
| Walkthrough | “You receive [ACTUAL COMPONENTS]. A typical section includes [REAL EXAMPLE].” | Genuine pages or app screens; remove private client information. |
| Demonstration | “This is how [ONE PROGRAM FEATURE] works.” | Human movement footage when form is being taught; otherwise an accurate product walkthrough. |
| One action | “See the details at [VERIFIED DESTINATION].” | One working link or QR code; include the verified price only if useful. |

**Evidence focus:** Verify product facts and any outcome claim separately. Show actual materials. Do not invent testimonials, client results, scarcity, or endorsements.

## Client and testimonial handling

Use client images, names, voices, results, or testimonials only within the permission granted for that specific use. Use the client's actual approved words for a testimonial and preserve the context that affects its meaning.

If suitable permission or authentic evidence is absent, use a neutral demonstration or product walkthrough. Keep private asset identifiers, internal links, client details, and personal health information out of public scripts, filenames, repository examples, and exported source notes.
